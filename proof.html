Two records. Never added together.

The single easiest way to make a model look good is to mix what it actually called before a fight with what it "would have" called looking backwards. So we keep them in separate boxes, and the code refuses to total them together.

Why the split is enforced in code, not just in copy. Every total on this page runs through one file, proof-gates.js, which throws an error rather than sum a list containing both kinds. A row whose origin we can't identify is counted as neither. There is a test for it — node tests/proof-gates.test.js.

How we measure ourselves, in plain English

No stats degree required. This is the whole method.

  1. We post a pick, and the clock starts
    Before a card, the model writes a row: which fighter, what percentage we give them, and — where we flagged a bet — the price on offer at that moment. The row gets a timestamp. That timestamp is what makes this a record instead of a story.
  2. The row is never edited again
    Once written, a pick cannot be changed or deleted. Not by us, not by a script, not by the database owner. The database itself rejects the attempt. So a pick that ages badly still sits there.
  3. The fight happens, and we grade it against what actually occurred
    Won, lost, or not settled yet. Nothing gets quietly dropped for being embarrassing — if a pick is missing from the archive it's because it was never posted, and you can check the count against the card.
  4. Money is counted flat: $100 a bet, every time
    Same stake on every bet, no chasing, no doubling up. A win at +150 pays $150; a loss costs $100. That's it. We use the price that was on record when we posted, not a better one we found later.
  5. A number only goes on screen once there's enough of it
    Small samples lie. Each headline number has a written rule for how many picks it takes before we'll show it, and those rules were set before we could see the answer. Until a rule is met, you get "still collecting" instead of a number. We'd rather show you an empty box than a flattering guess.
  6. Simulations stay labelled as simulations
    Re-running the engine over old fights is a useful sanity check and nothing more — the model gets to be smart about a world that already happened. Those rows are marked, boxed off, and never added to the live record.

What this page will not tell you

Being upfront about the limits is part of the point.

  • Whether you'll make money
    A record is a record. A 70% pick still loses 3 times in 10, and a good run of results can happen to a bad model. Nothing here is betting advice.
  • That we're beating the market
    We're not claiming that, because we can't yet — the price test above has a rule attached and it hasn't been met. The market's price is right more often than any model. Any edge lives at the margins.
  • That the simulated numbers predict the live ones
    They routinely don't. That gap is the single most common way a model looks better on paper than in practice, which is exactly why the two records are kept apart here.
