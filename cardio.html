Methodology

The Cardio Score

A single number that captures how a fighter performs in the second half of fights — when output drops, accuracy fades, and decisions get made. Built from per-round strike data across every UFC fight.

What it measures

Output sustain in deep waters

Every UFC fighter throws strikes at some pace in round one. The question that decides most fights past round two: do they keep throwing at that pace, or does their output collapse?

The cardio score combines two things. First, the change in strike volume from round one to round three and beyond. Second, the change in accuracy over the same window. A fighter who keeps throwing AND keeps landing as the fight goes deep gets a positive score. A fighter who slows down, loses accuracy, or both gets a negative score.

The formula

How the number gets built

Cardio composite
Output change R1 → R3+ + Accuracy change R1 → R3+
Both components are expressed as percentage changes from the fighter's round-one baseline. Capped at +150 / −150 to prevent low-volume fighters from scoring artificially high off tiny sample math.
Pull every R3+ fight in the window

For each fighter, we collect their last five fights that reached round three or later. Fights ending in R1 or R2 contain no cardio signal and are excluded.

Aggregate strike data per round

Across those fights, we sum significant strikes attempted and landed in round one (the baseline) and rounds three through five (the deep-water window).

Compute the change for each component

Output change = (R3+ strikes per minute − R1 strikes per minute) ÷ R1 strikes per minute × 100. Accuracy change is computed the same way using landed/attempted ratios. Both expressed as percentages.

Sum and cap

The two components add together. Range is hard-capped at +150 (extreme positive) to −150 (extreme negative), because larger numbers mean the fighter started so quietly in R1 that the math is unreliable.

Confidence tiers

How much data backs each fighter's score

Not every fighter has the same depth of cardio data. A welterweight with eight five-round wars has a far more reliable score than a heavyweight whose last three fights ended inside two minutes. We surface that with three tiers:

High confidence

30+ minutes

30 or more minutes of round-three-plus data. Score is reliable, treat it as a real signal.

Limited data

15–30 minutes

Some R3+ history but small samples. Score is directional only — flagged as limited on fight cards.

No reading

Under 15 min

The fighter rarely goes deep. That itself is a signal — they tend to finish fights or get finished early.

Weight class matters

Same fighter, different cardio

A fighter cutting from welterweight to lightweight is depleted on fight night. The same fighter moving up to middleweight is rehydrated and fresher than usual. Their cardio looks different at each weight — and the data confirms it.

Our cardio score is computed per weight class. When you see a fighter's cardio number on an upcoming fight card, it's specifically their cardio at that fight's weight class. If they don't have enough deep-water history at that weight, we fall back to their career cardio across all weights and flag the row as limited data.

Examples we've found in the data: Alex Pereira shows +128 at light heavyweight and +3 at middleweight. Charles Oliveira shows different numbers at lightweight than featherweight. Kamaru Usman's career score overstates his welterweight cardio because his lightweight days inflated it. The weight-class split surfaces signal that career averages destroy.

Honest limitations

What the score can't tell you

Damage vs. fatigue look the same in the data

A fighter who stopped throwing in R3 because they're being controlled or hurt looks identical to one who stopped because they're tired. The score can't distinguish those cases — it just measures output. Read the score alongside the fight context.

It only counts fights that reached R3

If a fighter finishes most of their opponents in R1, they'll show up as "no reading" — and that's correct. We're measuring what happens in deep waters, not predicting whether the fight will get there.

Round duration is approximate

UFCStats doesn't expose per-round duration on the data we scrape, so we treat every round as a full five minutes. The final round of a finished fight is shorter, which slightly inflates per-minute stats for finishers. Effect is small and applies evenly across fighters.

Recent five only

The score uses a fighter's last five deep-water fights. A fighter whose cardio improved or declined recently won't have that change reflected until they've had several R3 fights at the new level. This is intentional — recent form is more predictive than career averages.
